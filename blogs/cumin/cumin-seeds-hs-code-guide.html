Cumin Seeds HS Code 0909: A Grade Guide for Importers

Buying cumin seeds from India involves more than agreeing on price — the HS code on your commercial invoice determines your import duty, and the grade terminology (Singapore Quality, Europe Quality, FAQ) varies enough between suppliers that mismatched specs are one of the most common sources of disputes. This guide covers both.

Cumin Seeds HS Code: What the Classification Means

Under the international Harmonised System, cumin seeds are classified under Heading 0909 — "Seeds of anise, badian, fennel, coriander, cumin or caraway; juniper berries." This sits within Chapter 09, which covers coffee, tea, maté, and spices.

At the 6-digit HS level, whole (not crushed or ground) cumin seeds are classified as 0909.31 — "Cumin seeds, neither crushed nor ground." Ground or crushed cumin is separately classified under 0909.32. A common point of confusion: the adjacent 0909.2x subheadings (0909.21 and 0909.22) cover coriander seeds, not cumin. Confirm your supplier is using 0909.31, not 0909.21, on the commercial invoice. The distinction matters: if your invoice describes whole cumin seeds but the HS code references the coriander subheading, you will face customs queries.

India's 8-digit export classification for whole cumin seeds is 09093100. This is the code your supplier should declare on the commercial invoice and that will appear on the shipping bill (India's export customs declaration).

How This Classification Affects Your Import

Three practical implications for buyers:

  • Import duty rate. The duty applicable to cumin seeds under HS 0909.31 varies by destination. The EU, GCC markets, USA, and Southeast Asian countries all apply different MFN (most favoured nation) duty rates. Some markets offer preferential rates for Indian spice exports under bilateral trade agreements — the HS code is how customs authorities look up which rate applies to your shipment.
  • Pesticide MRL compliance. For EU, UK, and certain other regulated markets, cumin imports are subject to maximum residue limit (MRL) checks for pesticides. The HS classification determines which regulatory category is applied. A misclassification can redirect your shipment to a different inspection regime.
  • Documentation requirements. A phytosanitary certificate is required for cumin seed imports in most destination countries. The commodity classification that appears on this certificate must match the commercial invoice — another reason to confirm the correct code before shipping.

Grade Guide: Singapore Quality, Europe Quality, and FAQ

These three trade grades appear across most Indian cumin exporters' product listings. They are not standardised by a single certifying body — they are market conventions that describe cleaning method, purity level, and suitability for different end markets.

Singapore Quality (SQ) — 98–99% purity, machine cleaned: The standard export grade for spice trade and wholesale distribution, particularly across Middle Eastern and Asian markets. Machine cleaned to remove gross foreign matter and undersized seeds. Moisture typically ≤8%. The name reflects the Singapore spice trade's historical influence as a price-setting and re-export hub; it does not mean the product must be exported via Singapore.

Europe Quality (EQ) — 99.5%+ purity, sortex cleaned: The premium grade, specified by buyers in the EU, UK, and regulated food markets. Machine cleaned and then sortex cleaned — a colour and weight sorting process that removes discoloured seeds, foreign matter, and off-specification seeds with far greater precision than machine cleaning alone. Admixture is typically held to ≤0.5%. EU pesticide MRL compliant. Moisture ≤8%. Buyers importing for retail, food manufacturing, or markets with strict pesticide monitoring should specify this grade.

FAQ — Fair Average Quality, 98%+ purity, machine cleaned: The trade benchmark grade — "fair average quality" of the season's cumin crop. Cleaned but to a less exacting standard than SQ or EQ. Admixture may run to ≤2%. Moisture ≤9%. Used primarily for oil extraction, industrial applications, and price-sensitive bulk buyers where visual grade and residue spec is less critical. The most cost-effective option per tonne.

A note on volatile oil content: cumin seeds' characteristic aroma and flavour intensity are linked to volatile oil content, which typically runs 2.5–3.5% depending on the crop season and origin. This is not directly tied to cleaning grade — an FAQ-grade cumin can have the same volatile oil content as an EQ-grade cumin from the same crop. If volatile oil content matters to your application, request a CoA that includes this parameter.

Documentation Required for Cumin Import from India

A standard commercial cumin shipment from India will come with the following documents. Confirm each is available before the shipment is booked:

  • Commercial Invoice: HS code (09093100), product description (cumin seeds, whole), grade, quantity, and unit price. The invoice establishes the customs value and classification.
  • Packing List: Quantity per bag or container, gross and net weights, packing format (typically 25 kg PP bags or as specified).
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill: For sea and air freight respectively. Destination customs will match this against the commercial invoice.
  • Phytosanitary Certificate: Issued by India's NPPO (plant quarantine authority) confirming the shipment is free from pests and plant diseases. Required for cumin import in most countries. Some destination markets specify additional treatment requirements — confirm with your country's plant quarantine or food safety authority before the shipment is arranged.
  • Certificate of Analysis (CoA): Ideally from a third-party NABL-accredited laboratory. Should cover purity, moisture, volatile oil content, foreign matter, and — for EU-bound shipments specifically — pesticide residue analysis against EU MRL limits.
  • Certificate of Origin: Required if you're claiming preferential duty under a trade agreement. Issued by a recognised chamber of commerce in India.

Common Errors to Avoid

Grade ambiguity on the purchase order. "Machine cleaned cumin" without specifying a purity percentage or admixture limit can mean very different things to different suppliers. State the grade name (SQ, EQ, FAQ) and the specific purity percentage you require. If the supplier's terminology doesn't match these standard grades, ask them to provide a spec sheet with exact parameters.

Assuming the CoA covers pesticide residues by default. A standard CoA covers physical and microbiological parameters. A pesticide residue analysis panel is a separate, additional test — and EU buyers need this explicitly. Request it when placing the order, not after the goods have shipped.

Mismatched form on the invoice. If you order whole cumin seeds but your invoice description is vague — "cumin" rather than "cumin seeds, whole" — customs may request clarification, particularly in markets that apply different duty rates or MRL regimes to whole seeds vs ground spice.
