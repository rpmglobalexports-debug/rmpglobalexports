Sesame Seeds for Canadian Buyers: How to Source from India with Better Cost Control and Reliable Supply

Canada has a strong market for sesame seeds across bakery, snack foods, ethnic foods, tahini, confectionery, foodservice, and natural food products. For many Canadian buyers, sesame is not a new ingredient. The challenge is usually not demand. The challenge is finding the right balance between price, quality, documentation, food safety, and reliable supply.

Many Canadian businesses buy sesame seeds through local wholesalers or ingredient distributors. This is convenient because the stock is already in Canada, delivery is faster, and the buyer avoids the import process. But as usage grows, local wholesale buying can become expensive. At that point, direct sourcing from India can become worth comparing.

India is one of the important origins for sesame seeds, and Gujarat is known for strong agricultural sourcing and processing networks. For Canadian importers and food businesses, sourcing sesame directly from India can help reduce ingredient cost, access specific grades, and build a more flexible supply chain.

Why Canadian Buyers Use Sesame Seeds

Sesame seeds are widely used in Canada by different types of food businesses.

Bakeries use sesame for buns, breads, bagels, crackers, and toppings. Food manufacturers use sesame in snack mixes, seasonings, sauces, tahini-style products, and ready-to-eat foods. Ethnic food distributors supply sesame to grocery stores, restaurants, and foodservice customers. Natural food brands may use sesame in health foods, seed mixes, and plant-based products.

Because sesame is used across many categories, buyers often need consistency in size, cleanliness, colour, taste, and packing. A small difference in quality can affect the final product, especially in bakery and tahini applications.

Common Sesame Seed Types Buyers Ask For

Canadian buyers may require different types of sesame depending on their end use.

Natural sesame seeds are commonly used in food manufacturing, grocery distribution, and general foodservice supply. These seeds retain their natural outer layer.

Hulled sesame seeds have the outer husk removed. They are often preferred for bakery, confectionery, tahini, and applications where a lighter colour and softer texture are needed.

Sortex-cleaned sesame seeds are cleaned using colour sorting technology to remove discoloured seeds and foreign particles. This is important for buyers who need better visual quality and consistency.

Black sesame seeds are used in bakery, Asian foods, snack products, and premium food applications.

For most Canadian buyers, the right product is not simply "sesame seeds." The right product depends on the final use, required purity, moisture level, colour, and packing format.

Why Direct India Sourcing Can Make Sense

Local Canadian wholesalers play an important role. They keep inventory, manage imports, handle customs, and supply smaller quantities. For a small buyer, this convenience is valuable.

But once a company starts using sesame regularly, direct sourcing can be worth comparing. The main reason is cost control.

When a buyer purchases locally, the final price may include importer margin, wholesaler margin, warehousing, domestic handling, and distribution costs. Direct sourcing from India can reduce some of these layers, especially for buyers who can purchase in larger quantities such as 500 kg, 1 MT, or more.

Buyers comparing suppliers should also confirm the specific sourcing origin within India — markets like Unjha in Gujarat are known specifically for sesame, cumin, and psyllium husk sourcing and processing infrastructure, not just the state generally.

Direct sourcing can also give buyers more control over:

  • origin
  • packing size
  • quality parameters
  • documentation
  • shipment planning
  • supplier relationship
  • long-term pricing

The goal does not always have to be replacing a current supplier immediately. Many buyers first use direct sourcing as a comparison. They check whether the landed cost from India is better than their current local buying price.

What Canadian Buyers Should Check Before Importing Sesame

Sesame is a food ingredient, so price should not be the only factor. Canadian buyers should review the supplier's ability to provide proper product details and export documentation.

Important points to check include:

Purity: Higher purity means fewer unwanted particles and better usability.

Moisture: Moisture control is important because high moisture can affect shelf life and quality.

Admixture/foreign matter: Buyers should confirm acceptable limits.

Cleaning level: Machine-cleaned and sortex-cleaned sesame may be required depending on the application.

Packing: Common packing options include 25 kg or 50 kg bags, but requirements can vary.

Documentation: Buyers may need invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, COA, and other shipment-related documents depending on their import process.

Food safety controls: Canada has specific food import requirements, and importers are responsible for ensuring that imported food is safe and compliant. CFIA has specifically reminded importers of sesame products to review food safety controls and ensure imported sesame products are not contaminated with Salmonella.

Sesame and Allergen Labelling in Canada

Sesame is also important from a labelling and allergen point of view. In Canada, sesame seeds are listed as a priority allergen, so Canadian food businesses need to handle sesame carefully when it is used in packaged products.

For buyers who supply manufacturers, bakeries, or retail brands, this means sesame handling, storage, cross-contact control, and labelling communication matter. A reliable supplier should be able to clearly identify the product, packing, batch details, and documentation so the Canadian buyer can maintain proper traceability.

Import Requirements Canadian Buyers Should Know

Canadian food importers generally need to understand requirements under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations. CFIA notes that general food import requirements include licensing, preventive controls, and traceability for most food product types and commodities.

For commercial importing, CBSA says businesses generally need a Business Number from the Canada Revenue Agency with an import/export account before importing commercial goods into Canada.

Most buyers work with a customs broker or freight forwarder to manage clearance, duties, documents, and delivery. For a first-time direct import, this support can make the process easier.

MOQ: Should Canadian Buyers Start with 250 kg or 1 MT?

Many small companies want to test direct sourcing with a lower quantity. A 250 kg trial can be useful for testing quality, documentation, shipping process, and landed cost. However, very small shipments can become expensive per kg because many export and import costs are fixed.

That is why 1 MT is often a more practical commercial starting point. At 1 MT, freight and documentation costs are spread over more quantity, making the landed cost more competitive.

A good structure is:

  1. Sample first for quality approval.
  2. Trial lot for first import testing.
  3. 1 MT+ commercial order for better landed-cost advantage.

This approach helps Canadian buyers reduce risk before committing to larger shipments.
