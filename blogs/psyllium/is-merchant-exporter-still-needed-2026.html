Is a Merchant Exporter Still Needed in 2026?

It's a fair question, and it deserves a real answer instead of a defensive one. Alibaba puts manufacturers a search away. Google Maps and LinkedIn make it easy to find a factory's export desk directly. Every merchant exporter's website says some version of "we add value" — which is exactly what you'd expect a business to say about itself, whether or not it's true.

So let's actually argue the other side first, honestly, before answering it.

The case for going direct

If you can find the manufacturer, verify them yourself, negotiate directly, and handle documentation on your own, you cut out a layer of margin. For a buyer with the time, the experience, and the risk tolerance to do that work themselves, direct sourcing can be the right call. Large importers with dedicated sourcing teams and existing India relationships often do exactly this — and they should. That's not a controversial claim, and a merchant exporter pretending otherwise isn't being straight with you.

That's the real case. Anyone telling you it doesn't exist is selling, not reasoning.

Where it actually breaks down for most buyers

The direct-sourcing case assumes a few things that are true for a large, experienced importer and often aren't true for everyone else:

That you can actually verify a factory from outside India. A polished export-desk email and a few product photos aren't verification. Confirming a manufacturer's real production capacity, actual certification status (not just a logo on a website), and genuine quality consistency across multiple lots usually takes either a site visit or a relationship with someone who's already done that legwork. That's not a service fee — it's the actual work of not getting a bad container.

That one relationship covers what you need. If you're sourcing a single product from a single reliable factory you've worked with for years, direct makes sense. If you're building a program across multiple agricultural commodities — say psyllium husk, cumin, sesame, and chia — direct sourcing means four separate manufacturer relationships, four QC standards, four documentation processes, and four points of failure instead of one.

That a factory's export desk is built for buyer service. A manufacturer's core business is manufacturing. Their export function usually exists to move volume, not to manage your specific relationship. When something goes wrong — a delayed harvest, a quality slip, a documentation error — your priority as a buyer competes with their production schedule and their bigger domestic accounts. A merchant exporter has no factory to run. Managing your order is the entire job, not a function competing with one.

That risk doesn't need absorbing. If a factory you sourced from directly has a bad season or a capacity crunch, that's now your problem to solve from scratch — new supplier, new vetting, probably a delay you didn't plan for. A merchant exporter working across multiple verified processors can often move your order to another source in the network without you starting over.

So, is it still needed?

For an experienced buyer with an existing India network, deep product knowledge, and the bandwidth to manage multiple direct factory relationships — often, no. That buyer has already built, in-house, what a merchant exporter offers as a service.

For a buyer sourcing from India for the first time, working across more than one commodity, or without the bandwidth to personally vet and manage multiple factory relationships — the honest answer is yes, and not because of tradition. It's because verification, consolidation, and risk absorption are real work, and someone has to do it. The only question is whether you do it yourself or pay someone whose full-time job it already is.

That's not a sentimental case for merchant exporters existing. It's a practical one: the value only holds if it's actually delivered — real verification, real responsiveness, real accountability when something goes wrong. A merchant exporter who doesn't do that work isn't worth the margin either.
