Writing a Psyllium Husk Specification: Five Places the Spec and the Lot Part Ways

Most psyllium specifications we are asked to quote against are written before anyone has seen a lab report for the material. That is normal: if you are launching a fibre supplement or qualifying a new supplier, you start from a monograph, a competitor's label and what your QA team can defend. The trouble is that a few of those numbers are decided by the crop and the processing floor, not by the supplier you pick.

Push harder on the supplier and those numbers do not move. What changes is that honest suppliers stop replying and the rest say yes to everything. Here are the five places we see specifications and real lots come apart, with the numbers behind them. For the full list of test parameters, see our psyllium husk quality parameters guide.

1. Lead Is a Soil Question, Not a Supplier Question

Psyllium is the husk of Plantago ovata, grown in north Gujarat and Rajasthan. Heavy metals in it come from the soil the crop grew in. Cleaning, de-husking and milling do not remove them.

A conventional 95% husk powder lot tested this year came back at 0.602 mg/kg lead, by ICP-MS at a NABL-accredited laboratory, with a limit of quantification of 0.010 mg/kg. That lot was perfectly normal: most buyer specifications allow up to 2 mg/kg. But we were recently asked for 0.020 mg/kg, thirty times tighter than that result.

If you need a number that low, three things follow:

  • It becomes a sourcing question about specific growing regions and specific lots, not a line you can simply write into a purchase specification.
  • You need lot-by-lot ICP-MS screening before purchase, with a limit of quantification at or below 0.010 mg/kg so the result means something.
  • Expect to reject lots, and build that cost into your price, because your supplier will.

Ask any supplier who agrees to a very tight heavy-metal limit for actual results from recent lots, with the method and the limit of quantification. A spec sheet that repeats your own number back to you is not evidence.

2. Steam Sterilisation Has a Minimum Batch Size

If your microbiological limits require sterilisation and you have ruled out ethylene oxide and irradiation, steam is what remains. (We compare the three methods in steam vs ETO vs gamma sterilisation.)

Steam sterilisation cannot be run on a sample. Processors will not run a batch below about 500 kg, and 2 MT is the more common minimum. We called roughly fifteen facilities before accepting that this is an industry floor, not a negotiating position.

Cost moves sharply with batch size: around ₹20 per kg at 500 kg, falling to around ₹5 per kg at 2 MT. On a small trial order, sterilisation alone can be several per cent of your landed cost.

For qualification, that means you cannot evaluate steam-sterilised material on a 200 g sample unless the supplier has run a full batch for another reason. If someone offers you a small steam-sterilised sample quickly and cheaply, ask which batch it was drawn from. Sterilisation also slightly deepens the colour of whole husk, so agree the acceptable shade range before production, not after.

3. Organic Means a Transaction Certificate, Not a Certificate on the Wall

A supplier can hold a valid organic certificate and still be unable to sell you certified organic material. What your QA team needs for an organic claim is a transaction certificate issued for the specific lot you bought, traceable back through the chain. The facility certificate alone does not do that.

Fully documented organic lots of Indian psyllium are scarce right now. Suppliers we work with expect the first properly certified lots from the 2027 crop, harvested in February and March, so realistically available from the second quarter of 2027. Background in why organic psyllium husk is hard to find and our 2026–27 crop calendar.

European buyers have a second deadline. The EU's recognition of India's organic system under the equivalence arrangement runs to the end of 2026 and covers unprocessed plant products only. From 2027, imports move to the compliance regime, so your Indian supplier needs certification by a control body recognised for India under the new rules. If you are planning an EU organic launch for 2027, confirm which control body certifies your supplier, and under which regime, before you commit to packaging.

4. Swell Volume Belongs to the Form You Are Buying

Swell volume is the test that most directly reflects what psyllium does in a product, and it is the one most often copied across from the wrong form. Grinding changes the structure of the husk, so a swell volume agreed for whole husk may not hold at 60 or 80 mesh, and vice versa.

If you are buying powder, specify the mesh and the swell volume together, and ask for the result on that exact grade. The same goes for particle size: "60 mesh" on its own is not a specification; "not less than 95% passing 60 mesh" is. See psyllium purity grades and mesh sizes.

5. Verify Certificates With the Issuer, Not the PDF

This is the one that cost us the most to learn. A buyer's QA team caught inconsistencies in a set of certificates we had forwarded in good faith, and they were right to stop the qualification. We now check every document against the issuing body's own records before it reaches a buyer.

  • FSSC 22000: only certification bodies licensed by Foundation FSSC can issue it. Licensed bodies are listed on fssc.com, with the scheme categories each covers and whether the licence is full or provisional.
  • GMP and other scheme certificates: search the certificate number on the certifier's own verification page, then check that the certifier itself is accredited on IAF CertSearch.
  • FSSAI: every Indian food licence can be checked on the FoSCoS portal by licence number.
  • US FDA: the FDA does not issue certificates of registration. A food facility registration is a number, not a document; certificates carrying an FDA logo come from private agents.
  • Kosher and halal: a genuine kosher certificate normally comes from a rabbinical agency, lists the specific products approved and is renewed annually. A three-year certificate in the format of an ISO management-system certificate deserves a closer look.

Ten minutes per supplier, done before anything goes to a buyer, saves weeks. Our CoA checklist covers the lot-level document.

A Specification You Can Actually Buy Against

If you are writing your first psyllium specification, these lines carry most of the weight:

  • Form and grade: whole husk or powder, purity percentage, and for powder the mesh with a percentage passing
  • Swell volume, stated for that exact form
  • Loss on drying, total ash, acid-insoluble ash
  • Light and heavy extraneous matter
  • Heavy metals, with the method and the laboratory's limit of quantification, not just a limit
  • Microbiological limits, and whether they apply before or after sterilisation
  • Sterilisation method, explicitly excluding anything you will not accept
  • Pesticide residues against a named standard, and ethylene oxide if that matters to your market
  • Who tests, and when: in-house at the plant, or a third-party ISO 17025 laboratory on the production lot before shipment

That last line decides whether the rest of the specification means anything.
