Psyllium Husk — For Buyers

Four Questions First-Time Psyllium Buyers Ask (and What They Reveal)

These four questions come up in almost every first conversation we have with a new psyllium buyer. Each one is a reasonable thing to ask — and each one points to something about Indian psyllium that the buyer couldn't have known before asking. Here is what we have learned from being on the answering side.

1. "Can you hit 0.020 mg/kg lead?"

A US buyer asked us this on an inquiry for 99% husk a few weeks ago. We did not say yes. We tested with both of our 99% processors, and the honest answer came back the same from each: no, not consistently. The best historical result either of them could point to was 0.030 mg/kg, and even that was a single-lot in-house number, not a third-party verified run average.

The reason is soil, not processing. A conventional 95% husk powder lot we had tested earlier this year came back at 0.602 mg/kg lead by ICP-MS at a NABL-accredited laboratory with an LOQ of 0.010 mg/kg. That lot was perfectly normal for the Indian market — most pharmacopoeial specifications allow up to 2 mg/kg. But below about 0.030 mg/kg, you move out of the range Indian psyllium reliably produces, into one where you need region-specific sourcing and lot-by-lot screening.

What the question reveals: the buyer has done their homework on what they want in the finished product, but the number they chose is below what the soil the crop grew in can hand them. The right follow-up is not "we'll try" — it is a frank conversation about what is actually available, and whether sourcing from a specific region at a premium is on the table. More on heavy-metal thinking in our spec guide.

2. "Can we try a 1 kg sample of your steam-sterilised lot?"

Yes, in theory. Not in a way that means anything.

Steam sterilisation cannot be run on a kilogram. Processors we work with will not run a batch below about 500 kg, and 2 MT is the more common commercial minimum. We called roughly fifteen facilities before accepting that this is an industry floor, not a negotiating position.

Cost moves with the batch: at 500 kg, sterilisation alone runs around ₹20 per kg; at 2 MT, it falls to about ₹5 per kg. If someone sends you a 1 kg steam-sterilised sample quickly and cheaply, the right question is: which production batch was it drawn from? If the answer is vague, you are looking at material that was treated separately for the sample — which tells you about the method, but very little about what will arrive in your container. More on this in our comparison of steam, ETO and gamma sterilisation.

3. "Your supplier has an FSSC certificate — does it cover psyllium?"

This one came from a buyer's QA team catching a supplier of ours with inconsistencies in a certificate pack we had forwarded in good faith. They were right to stop the qualification. We now check every document against the issuing body's own records before it reaches a buyer.

What matters: FSSC 22000 is issued under scheme categories. A certificate can be valid for the facility and still not cover the product category you are buying. Only certification bodies licensed by Foundation FSSC can issue these certificates; the licensed bodies are listed on fssc.com, along with the scheme categories each body is licensed for and whether the licence is full or provisional. For an Indian food-grade processor dealing with dry plant materials, you want Category CI (processing of perishable plant products). Confirming the certificate number, the certifier's licence status, and the scheme category takes about ten minutes.

Three other certificates deserve the same treatment: FSSAI (verify on the FoSCoS portal by licence number), kosher (annual single-agency certificates with product-specific scope, not three-year ISO-style formats), and anything calling itself a "US FDA certificate" — the FDA does not issue certificates of registration. Our CoA checklist covers the lot-level document.

4. "Can you send a sample before I decide?"

Yes — and the sample is the easy part. The harder question, for both sides, is whether the sample represents the lot that will actually ship.

A sample pulled from an existing lot in stock proves the material is real. It does not prove that the lot in your next PO will be the same one. For material that will be sterilised or milled to order, or for buyers whose QA runs a full panel on every production lot, the only sample that tells the truth is one drawn from the production lot immediately before dispatch — the same lot your container will carry. Agree that up front, in writing, and the first shipment becomes a confirmation rather than a surprise.

What this question reveals: the buyer is used to being burned by bait-and-switch sampling. The right answer is not just yes; it is a specific sampling protocol the supplier will commit to in the PO.

A Short Note to First-Time Buyers

None of these questions is wrong to ask. They come up because the people asking are trying to protect their brand, their QA process and their launch timeline, and that is the right instinct. What is different in Indian commodity trade is that honest answers often carry a cost — a trip home from a trade show empty-handed, a sample that costs more than the material it represents, a certificate pack that takes longer to verify than to produce. A supplier willing to give you the slow answer is usually the one worth working with.
