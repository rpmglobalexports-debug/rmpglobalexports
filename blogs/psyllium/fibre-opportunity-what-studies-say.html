The Fibre Opportunity: What Peer-Reviewed Studies Say About the Next Five Years

Three things have converged in the last eighteen months that make this a genuinely unusual moment to be building a fibre brand. None of them is marketing hype. All three are in peer-reviewed literature or in data from the national surveys governments use to write dietary policy. Here is what the studies actually say.

1. The Fibre Gap: About 93% of US Adults Fall Short

An analysis of the 2013–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), presented by Derek Miketinas and colleagues at Texas Woman’s University, found that only about 7% of US adults meet recommended fibre intake— 5% of men and 9% of women. Average intake sits at 9.9g per 1,000 kcal for women and 8.7g for men, against a recommendation of 14g per 1,000 kcal. In absolute terms, the daily targets are 25g for women on a 2,000-calorie diet and 38g for men on a 2,500-calorie diet.

This is not a new finding. The Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee has flagged fibre as a “nutrient of public health concern” in every cycle since 2015. A 2021 clinical review in Nutrients (Reynolds et al., “Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025: Pulses, Dietary Fiber, and Chronic Disease Risk”) called for clearer policy action, citing the association between low fibre intake and elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer, and all-cause mortality.

The practical consequence for anyone building a fibre brand: the addressable population is not a niche of health-conscious consumers. It is more than nine out of ten adults in the largest consumer market in the world, with policy-level recognition that the gap matters.

2. The GLP-1 Wave: 12.4% of US Adults Now Need Fibre For a Different Reason

According to the Gallup National Health and Well-Being Index (2025 release), 12.4% of US adults report using a GLP-1 receptor agonist such as Ozempic, Wegovy, or Mounjaro — up from 5.8% in early 2024. Use has roughly doubled in eighteen months. Highest adoption is in the 50–64 age bracket (17.0%), with 15.2% of women and 9.7% of men reporting current use.

At the same time, peer-reviewed data on the side-effect profile has become clear. A 2024 review in Diabetes, Metabolic Syndrome and Obesity (Dovepress) consolidated findings from a real-world cohort of over 10,000 GLP-1 users:

  • Constipation: 30.4%
  • Diarrhea: 32.7%
  • Nausea or vomiting: 23.4%

A separate meta-analysis of 48 randomised controlled trials covering 27,729 participants reported nausea in 21.4% of patients. Nearly half of GLP-1 users discontinue therapy within a year, with physicians citing persistent GI symptoms as a leading reason.

The clinical consensus in the same review on managing these symptoms begins with a non-pharmacological recommendation: adequate hydration and fibre intake. This is not a brand's claim. It is in the published consensus guidance for a drug class that 12.4% of US adults now use. Extrapolating against a population of roughly 370 million, that is on the order of 45 million people who have been medically told that fibre is part of their therapy.

3. Psyllium Has the Strongest Peer-Reviewed Cardiovascular Evidence of Any Single Fibre

A dose-response meta-analysis published in Genes & Nutrition in December 2025 (41 randomised controlled trials, 2,049 participants) reported a weighted mean difference of -8.55 mg/dL in LDL cholesterol (95% CI -12.92 to -4.19, p<0.0001) among psyllium users compared with controls. At doses above 10g per day, the effect strengthened to -11.06 mg/dL. The authors flagged high heterogeneity across trials, but the direction and significance of the effect were consistent.

Psyllium is one of the few food ingredients the US FDA has authorised a health claim for: that consuming 7g per day of soluble fibre from psyllium husk, as part of a diet low in saturated fat and cholesterol, “may reduce the risk of heart disease.” The FDA has only ever granted about a dozen such claims. The European Food Safety Authority has issued comparable claims on bowel function and cholesterol maintenance.

For a brand-builder, this matters because of what you can say on-pack. Most ingredient claims in the fibre space are soft (“supports digestive health”). Psyllium’s regulator-backed claims are specific and defensible, which simplifies both the marketing story and the retailer buyer’s compliance review.

4. The Market Forecast Is Not Hype, But It Is Not Peer-Reviewed Either

Industry research firms place the global psyllium market at USD 660.7 million by 2030, with a CAGR of 9.7% between 2024 and 2030 (Grand View Research). Independent estimates from Mordor Intelligence and TechSci Research cluster around the same figure. Broader fibre-supplement growth is projected at 10–11% CAGR through 2030. The drivers cited by all three firms are the same: chronic disease prevalence, consumer shift toward functional foods, regulatory approvals that validate claims, and the clean-label ingredient preference.

A word of honesty: market forecasts are industry estimates, not peer-reviewed research. They are the best available numbers for sizing an opportunity, but they are not the same category of evidence as the clinical findings above. Treat them as directional, not deterministic.

5. What the Convergence Means for a New Fibre Brand

Put the four findings next to each other:

  • An established, chronic deficit in the world’s largest consumer market, with policy acknowledgement that it matters.
  • A new, fast-growing patient population of ~45 million US adults whose treatment guidance explicitly includes fibre.
  • An ingredient category (soluble fibre, psyllium in particular) with regulator-authorised health claims most functional foods cannot match.
  • Market research showing roughly 10% annual growth through 2030.

None of this says that launching a fibre brand is easy. Brand-building, distribution, and formulation are hard problems independent of the category’s underlying demand. What it does say is that the conditions underneath the category — the actual unmet need and the regulatory tailwind — are better aligned than they have been at any point in the last decade. Most consumer categories do not have this kind of alignment: large unmet need, growing new-population demand, regulator-backed claims, and double-digit forecast growth all at once.

For founders considering this space, the question is not “is the opportunity real” — the data above answers that. The question is which form factor (powder, capsule, flavoured drink mix, pressed-wafer, functional food insertion), which price point, and which channel strategy best matches the segment you want to serve. GLP-1 users look nothing like the historical fibre-supplement buyer. The underserved 93% of adults look like everyone. The next five years will reward brands that make honest formulation choices and build around specific, documentable claims.
