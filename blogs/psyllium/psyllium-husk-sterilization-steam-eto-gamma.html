Psyllium Husk Sterilization: Steam vs ETO vs Gamma

Raw psyllium husk is a field crop, dried in the open across north Gujarat and Rajasthan. For many food uses its natural microbial load is acceptable. For supplement brands and contract manufacturers with tight yeast, mould and pathogen limits, it usually is not, and the husk needs a kill step. Which method you choose matters as much as whether you sterilize at all, and it is the part of a psyllium specification buyers most often leave vague.

We learned this the practical way. Working through a whole-husk qualification for a North American supplement buyer this season, we called roughly fifteen sterilization facilities across Gujarat to understand what each method can and cannot deliver. This guide is what we found, written for the person who has to sign off on the specification.

Why Raw Psyllium Husk Often Fails Supplement Micro Specs

Psyllium is harvested in February and March, threshed, and handled in bulk before it reaches a processor. Cleaning, winnowing and grading remove sand, dust and light particles, but they do not reduce the microbial load. Supplement specifications commonly set limits on total plate count, yeast and mould, and a panel of pathogens. Some buyers set yeast and mould as low as 1,000 CFU/g and add specific pathogens such as Clostridium perfringens, Bacillus cereus and E. coli O157:H7 that are not on a processor's routine test list.

If your specification looks like that, assume conventional husk will need sterilization, and say so in the purchase order rather than discovering it at the COA stage.

Steam Sterilization

Steam is the method most supplement and food buyers prefer, because it leaves nothing behind. There is no residue to test for and no labelling consequence in the major markets.

The catch is scale. Across the facilities we contacted, the consistent answer was that a reliable, validated result needs a batch of at least about 500 kg. Below that, the equipment cannot hold time, temperature and moisture evenly enough to guarantee the microbial outcome. The per-kilogram premium also falls sharply with volume: a 2 MT batch costs far less per kilogram to sterilize than a 500 kg one, because the fixed cost of a run is spread over more material.

Steam can also slightly deepen the colour of whole husk. If your specification describes appearance, for example "pinkish-beige flakes", agree in advance how much colour shift is acceptable after sterilization. It is far easier to align on this with a photo before production than to argue about it after.

Ethylene Oxide (ETO)

ETO fumigation works on small lots and is inexpensive, which is why it remains common in some supply chains. For Europe, it is not an option. The use of ethylene oxide is banned in the EU, and imported food is tested for residues of ethylene oxide and 2-chloroethanol against very low limits.

This is not theoretical for psyllium. During the 2020–21 EU ethylene oxide incident, which began with Indian sesame seeds, contaminated psyllium husks were among the commodities identified. EU enforcement has since tightened, and many European buyers now ask for an ETO residue result on every lot regardless of the declared treatment.

Outside the EU, rules differ by market and product category. If a supplier offers ETO-treated husk for North America, have your regulatory team confirm that psyllium falls within an approved use before you accept it, and require a residue test either way.

Gamma Irradiation

Gamma leaves no residue and handles large volumes well, but it brings two complications for psyllium.

First, approvals are category-specific. In the United States, 21 CFR 179.26 permits irradiation of dry aromatic vegetable substances used as flavouring, such as culinary herbs, seeds and spices, up to 30 kGy. Canada's approved list covers whole and ground spices and dehydrated seasoning preparations. Psyllium husk is a fibre ingredient, not a flavouring or spice, so it does not clearly fall within either approval. Do not assume irradiated psyllium is permitted in your market; confirm it.

Second, labelling. Where irradiation is permitted, both countries require the Radura symbol on the irradiated food, together with a statement that it has been irradiated. Many brands will not accept that on their label. The facilities we spoke with also quoted a minimum of about 1.2 MT per run.

The Sampling Problem Nobody Mentions

Here is the issue that caught us out. A buyer's QA team reasonably wants to test a pre-shipment sample of sterilized husk before approving a large order. But if steam cannot be validated below about 500 kg, you cannot produce a representative 200 g steam-sterilized sample on its own. And a sample sterilized by a different method, such as ETO, does not tell you how the steam-treated production lot will perform.

The workable sequence is:

  1. Agree the full specification, including the sterilization method and microbial limits with test methods.
  2. Issue a conditional purchase order.
  3. The supplier produces and sterilizes an initial batch of at least 500 kg.
  4. That batch is tested by a third-party laboratory and a sample drawn from it goes to your QA team.
  5. Once the sample is approved, the balance of the order is produced the same way.

It costs the supplier a production-scale batch up front, which is why a conditional commitment from the buyer is fair at this stage. It is also the only way the sample you approve genuinely represents what you will receive.

What to Put in Your Specification

  • Sterilization method, stated explicitly: steam, or "no ETO, no irradiation".
  • Microbial limits with test methods, including the pharmacopoeia edition if you work to USP or Ph. Eur.
  • Residue requirement: ethylene oxide and 2-chloroethanol not detected, or below your market's limit, on every lot.
  • Appearance tolerance after treatment, especially for whole husk.
  • Sampling rule: the approval sample must come from the sterilized production batch, not a separate pilot lot.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Does psyllium husk need to be sterilized for export?

Not always. Many food applications accept conventional psyllium husk. Supplement and pharmaceutical buyers with tight yeast, mould and pathogen limits usually need a sterilized grade, and that requirement should be written into the purchase order.

2. Which sterilization method is accepted in the EU for psyllium husk?

Steam sterilization is the accepted route. The use of ethylene oxide is banned in the EU, and imported food is tested for ethylene oxide and 2-chloroethanol residues. Irradiation is permitted only for restricted food categories.

3. Can psyllium husk be irradiated for the US or Canadian market?

US and Canadian irradiation approvals are category-specific and cover spices, seasonings and similar flavouring substances. Psyllium husk is a fibre ingredient and does not clearly fall within them, so confirm with your regulatory team before accepting irradiated product. Where irradiation is permitted, the Radura symbol and an irradiation statement are required on the label.

4. What is the minimum batch size for steam sterilized psyllium husk?

In our experience across Gujarat facilities, a reliable, validated steam sterilization result needs a batch of about 500 kg or more, with the cost per kilogram falling significantly at 2 MT and above.

5. Why can't I get a small steam sterilized sample before ordering?

Steam sterilization cannot be validated on a few hundred grams, so a representative sample has to be drawn from a production-scale batch of at least about 500 kg. The usual approach is a conditional purchase order, an initial sterilized batch, third-party testing, and QA approval of a sample from that batch before the balance is produced.
